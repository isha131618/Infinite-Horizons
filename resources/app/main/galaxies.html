Galaxies


Galaxies are immense cosmic structures composed of billions or even trillions of stars, along with planets, gas clouds, dust, and dark matter.

An Overview on Galaxies


These celestial cities are held together by gravity, often revolving around a supermassive black hole at their center. They formed around 13.5 billion years ago, emerging from the primordial gas of the early universe. Over time, gravity pulled matter into denser regions, giving rise to the first galaxies. These galaxies have evolved through mergers, star formation, and cosmic interactions, shaping the universe as we see it today.


Types of Galaxies


Spiral Galaxies

Spiral galaxies are among the most visually striking, with their beautiful, rotating arms stretching outward from a bright central core. These arms contain vast amounts of gas and dust, making them fertile regions for new star formation. The Milky Way, our home galaxy, is a classic example of a spiral galaxy, with its massive, curving arms packed with young, hot stars. The spiral structure itself is maintained by the galaxy’s rotation and density waves, which act like cosmic traffic jams, compressing gas and triggering new star births. The Andromeda Galaxy, our nearest large neighbor, is also a spiral galaxy and is on a slow-motion collision course with the Milky Way, expected to merge with it in about 4.5 billion years.


Elliptical Galaxies

Elliptical galaxies have a smooth, featureless appearance, ranging in shape from nearly circular to elongated ovals. Unlike spiral galaxies, they lack defined arms and are typically composed of older stars. This is because they contain little gas or dust, the essential ingredients for forming new stars. As a result, they appear reddish in color due to the dominance of older, cooler stars. Some elliptical galaxies, such as Messier 87, are enormous, containing thousands of globular star clusters and hosting some of the most powerful supermassive black holes in the universe. Elliptical galaxies are often found at the centers of galaxy clusters, where frequent galactic collisions and mergers have smoothed out their structure.


Irregular Galaxies



Eros is a near-Earth asteroid with an elongated shape, measuring about 34.4 kilometers (21.4 miles) in length. It was the first asteroid to be orbited and landed on by a spacecraft—NASA’s NEAR Shoemaker in 2001. Eros belongs to the S-type asteroids, meaning it is composed mainly of silicate rock and nickel-iron. Scientists study Eros to understand the composition of asteroids that could potentially impact Earth. Its surface is covered in craters and boulders, with some features suggesting it has experienced violent collisions over time.


Lenticular Galaxies

Lenticular galaxies serve as a bridge between spiral and elliptical galaxies. They have a central bulge and a surrounding disk, similar to spiral galaxies, but lack the well-defined arms characteristic of spirals. Their appearance is often smooth, resembling elliptical galaxies, yet they contain more dust and gas than ellipticals, allowing for some star formation, though at a much lower rate. Lenticular galaxies are often found in densely populated regions of space, where interactions with other galaxies have stripped away much of their spiral structure while leaving their disks intact.

Galaxies in the Cosmic Web


Galaxies do not exist in isolation but are instead part of vast cosmic structures known as galaxy clusters and superclusters. The Milky Way is a member of the Local Group, a small cluster containing about 50 galaxies, including Andromeda and the Magellanic Clouds. The Local Group itself is part of a much larger structure called the Virgo Supercluster, which contains thousands of galaxies. On an even grander scale, the universe is organized into a cosmic web, where galaxies and galaxy clusters are connected by immense filaments of dark matter and gas, shaping the large-scale structure of the cosmos.
